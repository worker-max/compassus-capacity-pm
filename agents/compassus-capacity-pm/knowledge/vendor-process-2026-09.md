**Two framing decisions that hold across every communication:**

- **The virtual round is a cut, not a formality.** Not every vendor in stage 3 reaches stage 4, and the emails
  say so explicitly. Vendors are told either way.
- **Vendors are told not to pretend.** The invitation states plainly that we know not every component is in
  production, and asks them to distinguish what is built from what is envisioned. **Blurring the two is
  positioned as costing them, not helping them.** This is the single most useful thing the process does, given
  the finding in the evaluation doc that coverage grids and narrative answers disagree.

## 2. What the virtual session must produce

Set out in the invitation, in priority order:

1. **A walk-through of the full process starting at the point of referral** — how capacity is understood, how
   the visit gets scheduled, and how the patient and the clinician are engaged along the way. *Seeing that end
   to end tells us more than any individual feature.*
2. **The development process** — how work gets prioritized, built and released.
3. **Timelines** — what is committed, what is planned, what is still open.
4. **The partnership** — how they see it taking shape if we move forward together.

Vendors choose their own attendees, with the instruction to bring **whoever can speak to all of this fully** —
people closest to the work rather than a formal presentation.

## 3. Approved communications

### 3.1 Advancement email — final template

> **Subject:** Compassus Capacity & Scheduling — next stage, and scheduling your session
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> [Vendor] has advanced to the next stage of our capacity and scheduling evaluation. Thank you for the work
> behind your questionnaire response — it earned this.
>
> **What's next: a two-hour virtual session**
>
> We'd like to spend two hours with your team for a deep dive and live demonstration of your product. **Cris,
> copied here, will contact you directly to find a time that works for everyone.** We've set aside **the weeks
> of September 21 and September 28** for these sessions, and Cris will offer you a range of slots across those
> two weeks.
>
> **What we're hoping to see**
>
> Our goal is to come away with a clear and honest understanding of where your product stands today.
>
> The most valuable thing you could show us is a **walk-through of the full process, starting at the point of
> referral** — how capacity is understood, how the visit gets scheduled, and how the patient and the clinician
> are engaged along the way. Seeing that end to end will tell us more than any individual feature.
>
> We understand from your questionnaire that not every component of this is in production today, and we don't
> expect you to pretend otherwise. Where something is built, show us the product. Where something is planned or
> still to be designed, walk us through how you envision it working. Being clear about which is which will serve
> you better with us than a seamless demonstration would.
>
> Alongside that, we'd like to understand:
>
> - **Your development process** — how work gets prioritized, built and released
> - **Timelines** — what's committed, what's planned, and what's still open
> - **The partnership** — how you see it taking shape if we were to move forward together
>
> Please bring whoever from your team can speak to all of this fully. We'd rather hear from the people closest to
> the work than sit through a formal presentation.
>
> **Looking further ahead**
>
> These virtual sessions are a further round of selection, not a formality. **A subset of the vendors we meet
> virtually will be invited to Nashville** for a half-day working session at our offices.
>
> We're currently targeting **the week of October 5**, and if you're able to hold some flexibility that week, it
> would help us move quickly. I want to be straightforward, though: **this may land in mid-October instead.**
> Coordinating a half day across both our team and yours takes some doing, and we'd rather find a date that
> works properly than force one that doesn't.
>
> We'll confirm invitations and firm dates once all the virtual sessions are complete, and we'll let everyone
> know either way.
>
> Cris will be in touch shortly. If anything would help you prepare, please just ask.
>
> Thank you again — we're looking forward to it.
>
> Best regards,
>
> [Your name] · [Title] · Compassus Home Health

**Drafting decisions worth preserving:**

- **"Glad" was removed deliberately.** Opening on the fact is stronger.
- **The scheduling sentence separates the two actions** — Cris contacts them, and here is the window she is
  working within. An earlier "we're holding the weeks of…" read as ambiguous.
- **The production-status paragraph sits immediately after the walk-through ask**, so it reads as permission
  rather than a caveat. Its last line does the real work.
- **Nashville is phrased as "hold some flexibility," not "hold the week."** Asking a vendor to block five days
  for a maybe is a large ask, and softening it makes the request more likely to be honored.

### 3.2 Decline email — final template

Written to apologize sincerely for **what the process asked of them** and for the disappointment — **not for the
decision.** Apologizing for the outcome reads as though the process was flawed, which invites pushback and
undercuts the decision.

> **Subject:** Compassus Capacity & Scheduling — our decision, and my thanks
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I'm writing with news I'm sorry to send. After a great deal of deliberation, we will not be moving forward with
> [Vendor] in the next stage of our capacity and scheduling evaluation.
>
> I owe you a real apology for what this process asked of you. Our questionnaire was long and genuinely
> demanding — it asked you to expose your roadmap, your gaps and your uptime history in a level of detail most
> evaluations never request. Your team answered it seriously and at length, and I know what that costs in hours
> that were owed to your existing customers. I'm sorry the outcome doesn't match the effort you put in.
>
> I also want you to know your response was read closely and in full — not skimmed, not scored by a spreadsheet
> alone. [**One or two specific sentences naming something real** — a capability that impressed the team, a
> candid answer you respected, an idea that changed how we're thinking about the problem.]
>
> Our decision came down to fit against a narrow set of requirements specific to how Compassus operates today.
> It is not a judgment on the quality of your product, your engineering, or your people.
>
> Thank you, sincerely, for the work behind your response. It was a heavy ask, and your team met it in full.
>
> With appreciation,
>
> [Your name] · [Title] · Compassus Home Health

**Drafting decisions worth preserving:**

- **No separate "the door is closed" paragraph.** The opening line already says it; a second paragraph restating
  it reads as over-explained. The finality is carried by the first line and by the absence of any invitation to
  follow up.
- **The closing names the effort, not abstractions.** An earlier version thanked them for "your time, your candor
  and your care" — too abstract. What the sender wanted to honor was **the sheer volume of work**, so the line
  names it.
- **Avoid:** *"we'll keep you on file," "we'll reach out if anything changes," "let's stay in touch."* They read
  as kindness but leave the file open, and vendors chase them for months.
- **Personalize the specific paragraph.** It is what separates this from a form letter, and vendors can tell
  instantly. One concrete sentence is enough — MedArrive's duration-measurement work, CareConnect's coverage
  marketplace, Axle's decision to pull their HCHB integration rather than ship something they didn't believe in.
- **Decide on debriefs before sending, not when asked.** If willing, add *"If a short call would be useful to
  your team, I'm glad to make time."* If not, say nothing — silence is cleaner than a soft no. Either way keep
  written feedback general; detailed comparative reasoning in an email travels.
- **Do not name who won** unless it has already been announced. It converts a gracious note into a comparison.

## 5. Working conventions for this workstream

**House style — non-negotiable:**

- **US spelling only.** Never British. (*organization*, not *organisation*; *modeled*, *labeled*, *normalized*,
  *color*, *fulfillment*.)
- **Direct and fluff-free.** No preamble, no hedging, no restating the question.
- **Never present a vendor's marking as our finding.** Where a coverage grid and a narrative answer disagree,
  report the narrative and flag the discrepancy.
- **Never present a submitted figure as verified.** Say "as submitted."

**Document design.** Deliverables for this workstream use a consistent system: **Newsreader** display /
**IBM Plex Sans** body / **IBM Plex Mono** for eyebrows, labels and figures; pine-teal `#1D5A50` accent on bone
`#F1F2EE` ground, with a full dark-theme token set. Word documents use Georgia headings / Calibri body, US
Letter.

**Two production lessons worth not relearning:**

- **`display:grid` on an `<li>` makes every child element *and text node* a separate grid item** — a bold lead-in
  and the prose after it land in different cells, pushing text into the number column. Use
  `position:relative` + `padding-left` with an absolutely positioned `::before` counter instead.
- **Never claim a document is a one-pager without rendering it at page size.** Pin `@page{size:letter
  portrait;margin:0}` with `.page{width:8.5in;height:11in}` and verify the page count from the produced PDF
  before delivering.

**Tooling notes for this container.** Headless Chromium at `/opt/pw-browsers/chromium-1194/chrome-linux/chrome`
handles `--screenshot` and `--print-to-pdf`. `libreoffice-writer` / `libreoffice-calc` and `poppler-utils` are
**not** installed by default and must be apt-installed with the sandbox disabled; `openpyxl` and npm `docx`
likewise need installing. A LibreOffice xlsx round-trip **drops defined names with external references** —
re-inject the original `<definedName>` elements into the output zip afterwards.
