Scheduling Initiative Whiteboard Session, August 13, 2026
Executive Summary
August 13, 2026
Purpose: validate the current-state scheduling process map and variable inventory against operational reality, then move from current state into target-state design.
Attendees
Colin — host and field-clinical/operations leader; author of the process maps and variable inventory; owns all map revisions coming out of this session
Laci — the operational system-of-record SME; answers essentially every "how does it actually work" question with HCHB-level specificity, and is the highest-density source in the session
Evan — Executive and initiative lead; frames the three-module target architecture, owns the vendor relationships, and draws the intake-tool analogy throughout
The session ran in two halves: a step-by-step audit of the current-state map (roughly the first 55%), then target-state design and bottleneck deep dives.
What was decided
1. The single current-state map gets broken into four for improved visuals. Consensus that one monolithic flow is helpful but difficult to fully view.  OhSplit into: (a) SOC / Recert / ROC / new-order scheduling, (b) routine visit scheduling, (c) the auth cycle, (d) missed-visit and exception recovery. Ideally layerable so they still read as one composite. Colin owns the redraw.
2. A three-module target architecture was agreed at high level: Capacity Management (the always-on database and envelope), Scheduling Engine (matching, care-team recommendation, routing), Patient Engagement (voice AI and text for readiness calls, reminders, rescheduling).
3. Capacity is Phase 1 — and Phase 1 is visualization only, no automation. "Just get the information in front of the branch leaders would be a huge check." Territory and service area land in the same phase, likely the same dashboard.
4. The capacity tool is the scheduling grid. The manually maintained spreadsheet branches update multiple times a day is not an artifact to be preserved — the capacity tool replaces it. Do not build two things.
5. Care-team-at-referral was accepted as the design pattern, replacing per-visit routing back to a scheduler.
6. Auth gets mapped only at its interface with scheduling. A deep map of the auth team's own workflow is not required — the generalized flow on our side is sufficient.
The seven takeaways that matter
1. The governing discipline of this project is separating HCHB constraints from actual requirements. This surfaced early and became the session's organizing idea. Several of the most painful constraints are Home Care Home Base design choices, not Medicare requirements: routing every physician order to a DCS for approval; hiding pending-auth visits from the clinician's calendar; blocking a nurse from handing a visit to her own LPN; blocking an RN from seeing her supervisee's schedule. The explicit warning — "we have to be really careful that we're just not reinventing Home Care Home Base" — came with the sharper corollary: some workflows shouldn't be automated, they shouldn't exist. "That workflow shouldn't exist to begin with for the scheduler."
2. Most auth pain is self-inflicted, and that is good news. The most valuable reframe in the meeting. Plans of care are written without regard to payer limits, and the team is then frustrated when auth doesn't materialize. Concrete: UHC gives 5 nursing visits and requires 4 of 5 completed plus supporting documentation before visit 6; Indiana Medicaid pays 30 days from the hospital discharge date, not the admit date, with 8 visits shared across PT/OT/ST; Ohio Medicaid caps similarly. "UHC was never going to give you more auth. We're not creating our plans of care based on the insurance." The data already exists — the auth team writes it into a coordination note at verification. Surfacing payer rules at plan-of-care creation is a high-value, low-complexity win, and it is a patient-care win, not only a throughput one: abrupt discharges happen because nobody planned for the real visit budget.
3. Auth and DCS QA are hard stops, and they sit upstream of the scheduler's queue. Two distinct chokepoints. Plan-of-care QA must clear before the first week of visits can be assigned — if the QA team is behind, an LPN sits idle and three days of visits get compressed into one. And before a referral even reaches scheduling it must pass eligibility, have pending auth keyed by the auth team, then route back to intake for final approval. "We know we have the referral, but it's just not in my workflow to schedule yet because it's stuck in auth." Add the per-discipline task explosion: the plan-of-care workflow fires once per discipline, so four disciplines produce four tasks, then four more at approval — eight tasks of clicking for a decision already made.
4. Invisible work is uncounted work — a capacity measurement problem, not a UX complaint. Pending-auth visits exist nowhere the clinician or the leader can see, and don't count toward productivity. A visit appears on Thursday's calendar on Wednesday afternoon. The scheduler holds it "in her head or on a sticky note." The line that captures the whole initiative: "If you can't see it, you can't plan." Compounding it, HCHB generates a pending-auth workflow every single day per patient — roughly 50 a day per scheduler — which trains schedulers to bulk-clear without reading, so the one that mattered gets cleared too.
5. Clinician autonomy is the adoption constraint, not a feature request. The failure mode is documented and specific: smart-scheduling tools get rejected because clinicians will refuse an assignment from a machine that they would have accepted from a scheduler. "A scheduler could have assigned that exact same thing to them, but the tool did it, and they're like, well, it must be broken." The design response agreed on: clinicians enter their own availability windows and preferences; the tool recommends and the human accepts; the framing is assistant, not controller — "the more we can make this feel to the clinicians that they've got an assistant instead of someone trying to take over." And it should give them capability they lack today: hand off a visit to their own LPN, see their supervisee's schedule, self-serve open visits (HCHB's Shift Finder pattern, already available and not turned on).
6. Three changes are unusually high-leverage relative to their complexity.
Discipline-role match — PTs holding routine visits that PTAs should take. Automate assignment to the paraprofessional by default with explicit opt-out ("they have to opt out versus opt in"). Two simultaneous returns: lower cost per visit and freed PT capacity for starts, which is exactly where growth is currently blocked.
Care team at referral — assign the full team (LPN, PTA, COTA, aide, OT) once, with a system recommendation and human approve/edit. This is the most common reassign task today, and it collapses the per-discipline task explosion.
Data-driven territory — there is no data behind territory design today; hand-colored maps and zip spreadsheets that nobody re-cuts because re-coloring is too much work. A live census and referral heat map by zip and discipline serves scheduling and growth. Note the local knowledge the system must encode: the Jacksonville bridge (one zip, two non-interchangeable sides) and the California interstate crossing window.
7. Patient engagement is the largest untapped clinician time-back, and it has hard constraints. Roughly 3,000 home-health clinicians spending about 30 minutes a day — unpaid, in the evening — confirming tomorrow's visits. Automating that is a work-life-balance and a productivity win simultaneously, plus it makes appointments feel official (email plus text plus an arrival range, never a hard time), enables four touchpoints instead of one, and eventually "your clinician is on the way." The constraints identified are real and need legal input: text and email consents are not signed until the SOC visit; California treats any non-manually-triggered outbound call as a robocall; Washington/Providence requires a safety screening script put in place after a clinician was killed in a patient's home, which means scripts vary by region; and there is a genuine judgment call about whether a patient's first contact with Compassus should be an agent.
Also material
Call-out recovery is unmanaged churn. No established process — all hands, stop workflow, non-clinical schedulers opening charts one at a time to triage clinical priority, then begging clinicians. It cascades into the following days' capacity. The ask: a "flare button" that triages by clinical priority (SOC, IV, wound, labs due, ortho) and recommends nearby coverage, with clinical sign-off retained.
The readiness call is inconsistently done and it causes harm. Growth's first touch doesn't happen everywhere; at least one scheduler refuses the call outright. Consequence given first-hand: a clinician shifted two visits to her PTA to make room for an SOC, nobody confirmed the patient was home, the patient was still in the hospital, and she lost half a day of income.
Incentives are a lever with caveats, and a mechanism already exists. Surge or marketplace pay for hard visits, bundling adjacent visits, first-to-accept. Note that this does not need inventing: HCHB non-visit-activity (NVA) coding already pays differentiated percentages of the unit or hourly rate, and branches already reach for it when they get desperate. Caveats raised: clinicians will learn to hold out for the higher offer; union and salaried/hourly populations cannot be incentivized the same way; and a meaningful share of clinicians are not money-motivated at all — their currency is finishing at 3:00.
Coordination notes are a workaround, but less fragile than assumed. They are titled and routed — a scheduler-titled note creates a scheduler task; a Point of Care visit alert forces clinician acknowledgment before the visit opens. Still a pattern built to compensate for missing workflow, and one to design out rather than replicate.
Vendor read: the top four or five candidates understand capacity and scheduling and the relationship between them; few have thought through patient engagement. Likely shape is one vendor for capacity plus scheduling with a voice AI layer bolted on. Nobody is close to the GPS-aware proactive re-time scenario — "that's like version 5."
Headcount implication was stated openly: if there are roughly 300 schedulers today, perhaps 100 in the target state — while deliberately preserving a human air-traffic-controller role with local knowledge for urgency and relationship-based coverage. One precision for the business case: that workload sits in the SOC/recert/ROC cycle, the per-discipline assignment burst after each evaluation, auth chasing, and exception recovery. It does not sit in routine visits, which are largely clinician self-managed once assigned.

A.7 Post-session corrections
The current-state record was corrected after the session by Colin. These corrections are substantive, not editorial, and Part B reflects them in full. They are logged here so the provenance of the current-state record is traceable.
Routine visit scheduling was materially in need of clarification post-session.
The corrected mechanism:
Routine visits are first plotted by the evaluating clinician at admission, not by the scheduler.
Each clinician plots frequency for their own discipline only. The admitting RN establishes the nursing plan of care and frequency; PT and OT establish theirs at their own evals, normally within 1-2 days.
The exception is a service the RN identifies beyond the referral: the RN plots HHA frequency (which remains RN-managed) and/or the initial eval visit for MSW or ST, whose clinicians then develop their own discipline plan of care.
Each discipline's frequency submission generates its own scheduler assignment task.
After the 485 is submitted, further orders are add-on / subsequent orders; those that change frequency generate additional scheduler workflow.
In steady state there is no scheduler workflow at all unless a visit must be reassigned. The assigned clinician may move any non-OASIS visit within the Medicare week (Sunday through Saturday), provided the target date stays inside the 60-day certification period from SOC.
The clinician's weekly planning logic — own capacity, clinical prioritization across their caseload, geographic grouping, and hard constraints (strict wound care, catheter, IV timing, patient preferences, competing home health and outside appointments) — is entirely undocumented and unassisted today.
Scope of auth mapping settled. Deep mapping of the auth team's internal workflow is not required; the generalized flow on our side is sufficient. Recorded as DE-06.
