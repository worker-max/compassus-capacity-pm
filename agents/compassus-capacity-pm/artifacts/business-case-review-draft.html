Capacity and Scheduling

Value levers and measurement requirements · review draft, second pass

A discussion document. It contains no figures by design. The first section sets out how this program creates financial value. The second sets out what we would need to request in order to size each item and to track it once underway.

Why scheduling is a financial systemFive characteristics of this business determine how the levers behave. Worth establishing before the list, because several items below read as counterintuitive without them.

  • F.1We are paid in two fundamentally different ways depending on the insurer, and the two respond in opposite directions to the same operational change. Traditional Medicare pays a fixed amount for a period of care regardless of how many visits we deliver above a minimum. Every other insurer pays for each visit delivered, but only with permission granted in advance.
  • F.2Most of our clinicians are paid for each visit they complete rather than by salary. This means efficiency gains do not automatically become savings. They become available capacity, and capacity only becomes money if we fill it.
  • F.3Clinician capacity is perishable. An open slot on tomorrow's schedule is like an empty seat on tomorrow's flight. If it is not filled, it is gone, and it does not carry forward.
  • F.4Several of the ways we currently lose money are deadline-driven and invisible until the deadline has already passed. By the time we can see them, nothing can be done.
  • F.5A branch is either capacity-constrained or demand-constrained, and the correct strategy is opposite in each case. Several levers below only create value in the first state, and would do harm in the second. The system has to know which state a branch is in.

Section one  ·  Value levers

L1

Admission ThroughputUPDATED

Starting more patients on service using the clinicians we already employ, by shortening the time it takes to confirm that an admission will actually happen.

  • L1.1The connection: earlier certainty about which admissions are real means capacity is redirected while the day can still be used, rather than discovered empty the following morning.
  • L1.2A clinician's open slot for tomorrow does not carry over to the next day. If it is not filled, that capacity is permanently lost.
  • L1.3Standardized welcome calls, made as early in the day as possible rather than waiting for office staff to work through a queue, are the single largest source of early warning we have.
  • L1.4Two things routinely change between a referral being accepted and care beginning. Anticipated hospital discharges are delayed, and patients who agreed to a next-day visit when sales confirmed frequently defer once the branch calls.
  • L1.5Both are discovered at the welcome call. The earlier that call happens, the more of the day remains to redirect that capacity to another admission rather than losing it.
  • L1.6This pattern is most pronounced at weekends. Sunday in particular carries a high rate of patients canceling or refusing the start-of-care visit and deferring to Monday.
  • L1.7Weekend admission performance has a disproportionate effect on the following week. A productive Saturday and Sunday materially changes Monday's available capacity and can determine whether a branch meets its admission goal for that week.
  • L1.8Our referral system is improving how many referrals we accept. This lever addresses what happens after acceptance and before care begins.
L2

Assessment Capacity ReleaseUPDATED

Freeing clinicians qualified to perform admissions, therapy evaluations, and other assessment visits from routine visit work, by shifting those routine visits to paraprofessional staff who have availability.

  • L2.1The connection: assessment-capable clinicians are the true constraint on admissions. Moving routine work off their schedules converts existing staff time into admission capacity without hiring.
  • L2.2Only licensed assessing clinicians can perform admissions, resumptions of care, recertifications, discharges, and therapy evaluations. A large share of routine visits can be performed by a paraprofessional.
  • L2.3When assessing clinicians spend their week on routine visits, the branch forfeits admission capacity it already has and is paying for.
  • L2.4Important condition: this only creates value when a branch is at capacity and at risk of declining referrals. If referrals are limited and clinicians are short of visits, shifting work away from them reduces their income and damages retention. That situation calls for a different strategy entirely.
  • L2.5The system therefore has to know which state each branch is in before recommending any shift. Getting this wrong in a demand-constrained branch would be actively harmful.
  • L2.6It also requires knowing paraprofessional availability accurately, which today is not visible in one place.
L3

Same-Day Schedule Recovery

Refilling a clinician's open time with comparable work when a patient cancels late, rather than losing that portion of the day.

  • L3.1The connection: an infrastructure that can identify and offer a suitable replacement visit quickly turns a lost afternoon into a delivered, billable visit.
  • L3.2When a patient cancels, rescheduling that patient is normally handled during the cancellation call. That is not the issue.
  • L3.3The issue is the gap left in the clinician's day. Filling it requires knowing immediately which other patients are due, which are geographically close, which have insurer approval in place, and which visits are flexible enough to move.
  • L3.4Occasionally the right answer is bringing a visit forward from later in the week, which exchanges a gap we cannot fill today for one we have several days to fill.
  • L3.5Because most clinicians are paid per visit, an unfilled gap is lost income for them rather than lost revenue for us. This connects directly to the retention lever below.
  • L3.6This becomes more difficult as scheduling becomes more efficient. A tightly planned week has less slack to absorb a cancellation, so this capability must accompany the optimization rather than follow it.
L4

Episode Payment ProtectionUPDATED

Preventing periods of care from closing below the minimum visit count Medicare requires for full payment, known as a LUPA.

  • L4.1The connection: visibility into a period's visit count while days remain converts an after-the-fact write-down into a correctable operational issue.
  • L4.2Medicare pays a fixed amount for a period of care, but only if a minimum number of visits occurs. Fall below it and the period is repriced to a much lower per-visit amount. This is the Low Utilization Payment Adjustment, or LUPA.
  • L4.3Most shortfalls are by a single visit, and typically because a visit was missed or moved rather than because fewer visits were clinically necessary.
  • L4.4We currently discover this after the period has closed, when no remedy exists.
  • L4.5This is a tracking and visibility problem rather than a clinical one.
  • L4.6An important boundary: we would never add a visit a patient does not require in order to avoid a LUPA. Federal auditors specifically examine that pattern, and the only appropriate recoveries are visits that were clinically indicated and lost operationally.
L5

Reassessment Window Compliance

Ensuring therapy reassessment visits occur inside their required windows, so that visits already delivered remain billable.

  • L5.1The connection: holding every episode deadline in one place, surfaced with lead time, prevents care we have already paid to deliver from becoming unbillable.
  • L5.2Therapy requires reassessment visits at defined points in an episode. If the reassessment does not occur within its window, visits already delivered cannot be billed.
  • L5.3This is the most damaging category of loss on this list. The work was performed, the clinician was paid for it, and the revenue is forfeited.
  • L5.4It is currently tracked manually by schedulers, competing for attention with a high volume of other daily tasks.
  • L5.5Structurally it is the same problem as the LUPA threshold: a deadline attached to an episode that no one can see approaching, discovered only after it has passed. One mechanism addresses both.
  • L5.6A useful question for the discussion: do we currently report how often this occurs, or what it costs us annually?
L6

Scheduling Administration Cost

The number of staff required to assign visits, once the repetitive portion of that work no longer requires human handling.

  • L6.1The connection: removing task volume that exists only because the system generates it reduces the headcount required to process it.
  • L6.2Schedulers currently spend the majority of their time processing a task queue rather than making scheduling decisions.
  • L6.3A single patient generates a separate task for each clinical discipline involved, and again at approval.
  • L6.4The function should not be eliminated entirely. Urgency, local knowledge, and coverage relationships still require judgment.
L7

Premium Labor AvoidanceUPDATED

Reduced spend on contract clinicians, per diem staff, and incentive payments made to employed clinicians for absorbing additional visits at short notice.

  • L7.1The connection: visibility into who has available capacity converts a reactive premium purchase into a planned assignment using staff already on the payroll.
  • L7.2Premium labor here means any spend above our standard visit rate to get a visit covered: contract clinicians, per diem staff, and non-visit activity payments used to incentivize employed clinicians to take on extra work.
  • L7.3When a clinician calls out early in the morning, no one can see who has room, so the branch either buys the most expensive coverage available or loses the visit.
  • L7.4One caveat worth stating plainly. Because most clinicians are paid per visit, the saving is the differential between our standard visit rate and the contract or incentive rate, not the full amount paid. In an organization with salaried clinicians this lever would be considerably larger.
L8

Clinician RetentionUPDATED

Reduced voluntary turnover, and the replacement cost that accompanies it, through a more predictable schedule, a more predictable income, and earlier visibility of clinicians who are struggling.

  • L8.1The connection: a stable weekly schedule produces a stable paycheck, and leadership visibility of productivity identifies a struggling clinician early enough to intervene rather than replace.
  • L8.2Because most of our clinicians are paid per visit, schedule volatility is income volatility.
  • L8.3Published research on home health nurses found that those with the most erratic week-to-week visit counts were significantly more likely to resign, and that stabilizing the schedule measurably reduced that risk.
  • L8.4The effect appeared only among full-time staff, which is consistent with the mechanism being income dependence rather than preference.
  • L8.5Risk is not confined to the first ninety days. It recurs at the six month and one year marks, and each has a different character. A clinician who has survived ramp-up may still leave when the income never quite reaches what was represented.
  • L8.6Leadership visibility of productivity does two things at once. It supports accountability, and it surfaces the early signs of a clinician who is struggling. The second is the one that keeps people.
  • L8.7Built correctly, this data supports and engages clinicians rather than only monitoring them. That distinction determines whether the tool is accepted or resisted.
  • L8.8Automated next-day visit confirmation removes a taxing task performed unpaid, in the evening, that has nothing to do with patient care.
  • L8.9It also removes a task many clinicians find genuinely difficult. Confirming visits well requires negotiation, and not every excellent clinician has the interpersonal strategies to secure the appointment times that make their day work.
  • L8.10A system that can negotiate a reluctant patient into a nine o'clock appointment anchors that clinician's entire day. The value of that, in both work-life balance and clinical efficiency, is difficult to overstate.
  • L8.11This is the same mechanism as L3, viewed from the clinician's side. Every unfilled opening is income a clinician expected and did not receive.
L9

Travel and Territory Efficiency

Fewer miles driven per visit, through better daily sequencing and territories defined by actual drive times rather than map boundaries.

  • L9.1The connection: territories and daily routes built on real travel times reduce reimbursable mileage and return productive time to clinicians.
  • L9.2Territories are currently drawn manually and revised infrequently. A day's visits are grouped by distance rather than by actual travel time.
  • L9.3One caveat that matters for the arithmetic. Because most clinicians are paid per visit, time saved in the car belongs to the clinician, not to the company. What we recover directly is reimbursed mileage.
  • L9.4The time returned to clinicians becomes available capacity, which is captured in L1 and L2. Counting it in both places would overstate the case.

Identified but not yet quantifiedEach is credible and deliberately carries no figure, because the data required to value it does not exist today.

U1

Clinician Recruitment Advantage

A hiring proposition improved by removing unpaid administrative burden and by making quoted earnings achievable in practice.

  • U1.1The evening confirmation burden is removed, which is a tangible difference a candidate understands immediately.
  • U1.2A candidate quoted an expected income is considerably more likely to achieve it when their schedule is protected and cancellations are replaced.
  • U1.3Left unquantified because we do not currently track time to fill a vacancy, offer acceptance rates, or cost per hire.
U2

Hospice Line Extension

Applying the same capacity and scheduling capability to the hospice business.

  • U2.1Our prior working session concluded that hospice requires additional rules rather than a separate system.
  • U2.2This would extend the return at limited incremental cost, and is deliberately excluded until home health demonstrates the result.
U3

Authorization Timing Write-Offs

Care delivered outside the window an insurer permits for retroactive approval, which cannot be billed.

  • U3.1Insurers allow only a short period in which an approval can be backdated. Care delivered outside that window is written off.
  • U3.2This is not currently measured anywhere. It could prove immaterial, or it could be the largest item on this list.
  • U3.3It is the first item worth measuring, precisely because we do not know which it is.

Section two  ·  Measurement requirementsBaseline data is required once, to establish current performance and size the opportunity. Ongoing data is what we would monitor thereafter to confirm the result. These are different requests: the first is a one-time extract, the second is a reporting commitment that needs an owner.

D1

Admission ThroughputUPDATED

Baseline

  • D1.1New patient starts per year, by branch and by month.
  • D1.2Time stamps at each step between accepting a referral and delivering the first visit: referral accepted, insurance verified, intake approved, welcome call completed, visit scheduled, visit delivered.
  • D1.3Time of day the welcome call is completed, and how long after intake approval it occurs.
  • D1.4How often an accepted referral does not convert to an admission on the expected day, split by cause: hospital discharge delayed, patient or caregiver deferred, and all other reasons.
  • D1.5Admission activity by day of week, including Saturday and Sunday, for at least twelve months. Weekend performance is a baseline requirement for this initiative rather than a detail.
  • D1.6Start-of-care visits refused or deferred by day of week, with Sunday reported separately.
  • D1.7The relationship between weekend admission performance and the following week's capacity and admission attainment.
  • D1.8Referrals declined, with reason, separating capacity-driven declines from all others.

Ongoing measurement

  • D1.9Median and worst-case time from referral acceptance to first visit, weekly.
  • D1.10Welcome calls completed before a defined hour of the day, as a proportion of the total.
  • D1.11Same-day deferral and refusal rate, by day of week.
  • D1.12Weekend admission attainment, and its effect on the following week.
  • D1.13Referrals declined for capacity, weekly, by branch.
D2

Assessment Capacity ReleaseUPDATED

Baseline

  • D2.1Visits by discipline and by visit type, separating assessment visits from routine visits.
  • D2.2Which clinicians are credentialed and available to perform assessment visits, by branch.
  • D2.3The proportion of assessing clinicians' weekly schedules occupied by routine visits that a paraprofessional could have performed.
  • D2.4Paraprofessional availability against routine visit demand, by branch and by week.
  • D2.5Whether each branch is capacity-constrained or demand-constrained, week by week, over at least twelve months. This determines whether the lever applies at all.
  • D2.6Branch-level referral volume against admission capacity, to establish how often each state occurs.

Ongoing measurement

  • D2.7Share of assessment-capable clinician time spent on routine visits, weekly.
  • D2.8Paraprofessional utilization against available capacity.
  • D2.9Branch state indicator, capacity-constrained or demand-constrained, weekly.
  • D2.10Admissions accepted that would previously have been declined.
D3

Same-Day Schedule Recovery

Baseline

  • D3.1Canceled and missed visits: volume, and how far in advance of the visit the cancellation occurred.
  • D3.2Cancellation reason, separating patient-initiated from clinician-initiated.
  • D3.3Of those cancellations, how many left a gap in the clinician's day that was never filled. This is the actual loss, and it is not captured in any current report.
  • D3.4Where a gap was filled, elapsed time from cancellation to replacement assignment.
  • D3.5How often a visit is brought forward from later in the week to cover a gap, and whether the resulting later gap was then filled.
  • D3.6Which visit types are genuinely flexible on timing, defined by clinical leadership rather than inferred from the system.

Ongoing measurement

  • D3.7Cancellations per week, and the proportion leaving an unfilled gap.
  • D3.8Average elapsed time from cancellation to replacement assignment.
  • D3.9Clinician capacity lost to cancellation, per branch, per week.
  • D3.10Visits moved forward to cover a gap, and how often this created a second unfilled gap.
D4

Episode Payment ProtectionUPDATED

Baseline

  • D4.1Frequency of periods closing below the LUPA threshold, by branch.
  • D4.2For those that fell short, the size of the shortfall in visits.
  • D4.3For shortfalls of a single visit, whether the visit was missed, moved, or held pending insurer approval.
  • D4.4Cost to deliver a period of care, so the loss can be stated as margin rather than revenue.

Ongoing measurement

  • D4.5Open periods currently tracking below the threshold, with days remaining.
  • D4.6Periods closing short in the prior month, with cause coded.
  • D4.7Proportion of shortfalls that were preventable, meaning a clinically indicated visit was lost rather than not required.
D5

Reassessment Window Compliance

Baseline

  • D5.1Therapy reassessments completed outside the required window, by branch and by discipline.
  • D5.2Value of visits written off as a result.
  • D5.3How far past the window the late reassessments typically fell, since one day late and three weeks late are materially different problems.
  • D5.4Whether the miss originated in scheduling, clinician availability, or documentation.
  • D5.5How reassessment deadlines are tracked today, and by whom.

Ongoing measurement

  • D5.6Reassessments due within the next two weeks, with status.
  • D5.7Proportion completed inside the window, monthly.
  • D5.8Write-off value attributable to missed reassessment windows, monthly.
D6

Scheduling Administration Cost

Baseline

  • D6.1Ninety days of scheduler task records with start and completion times, grouped by task type.
  • D6.2Current scheduling headcount by branch, with fully loaded cost per role.
  • D6.3Distribution of task volume across those staff, since averages conceal where the load actually sits.
  • D6.4Which task types are non-actionable, meaning they are opened and closed with no action taken.

Ongoing measurement

  • D6.5Tasks per scheduler per day, and time per task type.
  • D6.6Proportion of tasks closed with no action taken.
  • D6.7Scheduling headcount relative to branch volume.
D7

Premium Labor AvoidanceUPDATED

Baseline

  • D7.1Contract clinician, per diem, and non-visit activity incentive spend for the trailing twelve months, by branch and discipline.
  • D7.2The proportion of that spend committed with less than twenty-four hours notice, which is the reactive portion this lever addresses.
  • D7.3Rate differential between our standard visit rate and the contract or incentive rate for an equivalent visit.
  • D7.4Frequency of visits going uncovered entirely rather than being covered at premium rates.

Ongoing measurement

  • D7.5Premium spend per branch, monthly, split between planned and reactive.
  • D7.6Visits covered at premium rates, weekly.
  • D7.7Visits uncovered, weekly.
D8

Clinician RetentionUPDATED

Baseline

  • D8.1Departures over the trailing twenty-four months, with date and length of service.
  • D8.2Departures grouped by tenure band: under ninety days, ninety days to six months, six months to one year, and beyond one year.
  • D8.3Replacement cost per clinician, by discipline.
  • D8.4Weekly visit counts per clinician for the trailing twelve months, which allows schedule stability to be calculated per individual.
  • D8.5Income quoted at hire against income actually earned at ninety days, at six months, and at one year.
  • D8.6Time clinicians spend on next-day visit confirmation, and when in the day or evening it occurs.
  • D8.7Visit start times by clinician, to establish how often a day is anchored with an early first visit.
  • D8.8Stated reasons for leaving, coded consistently rather than captured in free text.

Ongoing measurement

  • D8.9Turnover rate monthly, by discipline and by tenure band.
  • D8.10Week-to-week variability in visit counts per clinician, as an early indicator of resignation risk.
  • D8.11Clinicians whose earnings are tracking below the income quoted at hire, at each of the three tenure marks.
  • D8.12Productivity trend per clinician, surfaced to leadership as a support signal rather than only as an accountability measure.

On the scale of this request

  • S.1Every figure in a business case is one of three things: measured, published, or assumed. At present too many of ours are assumed.
  • S.2This list is what converts the assumed figures into measured ones.
  • S.3The majority already exists within systems we own and can be produced as extracts. A smaller number, particularly around canceled visits, reassessment windows, and weekend admission patterns, is not captured anywhere today and would require new instrumentation.
  • S.4Those gaps are notable in themselves. They are the areas where we currently cannot see the loss we are attempting to address.
