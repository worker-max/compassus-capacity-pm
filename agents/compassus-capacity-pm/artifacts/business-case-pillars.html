The Six PillarsEvery vendor walks through the same six doors.

Capacity must be solved before scheduling. The first job is not to optimise anything: it is to make capacity measurable. Any vendor that can’t hold up each pillar below, on our data and under our payer mix, doesn’t make the shortlist.

I
Gate · foundation

Capacity Visibility

Capacity and scheduling are two functions forced through one spreadsheet, with no forward view. Work waiting on authorization doesn’t show up in any count.

Waterfall: none directly. Every other pillar depends on it.

Vendor must prove
  • Computes maximum deliverable visits per week vs. delivered, by discipline and branch
  • Counts work held in pending authorization as real demand
  • Handles ramp: new clinicians aren’t counted at full capacity (gap C-11)
  • Keeps history, so it can trend, forecast and prove ROI
The lock question

“Show us next week’s capacity for one branch, by discipline, including work stuck in authorization. Use our data, not a demo tenant.”

II
Waterfall

Demand Conversion

Capacity is perishable: tomorrow’s start-of-care slot either fills or it’s gone. Authorization is the real bottleneck, and it sits upstream of the scheduler’s queue.

Recovered admissions · $28.8K / branch (Moderate)

Vendor must prove
  • Reserves start-of-care capacity so routine volume can’t eat it
  • Shows authorization turnaround by payer at the point of decision
  • Shortens the time from referral to a yes or no, so a declined slot can be refilled
The lock question

“Where does authorization live in your model, and how does one pending auth change tomorrow’s capacity?”

III
Waterfall

Payer & Episode Integrity

Payer rules are written down days before they’re needed, but never reach the plan of care. 81.7% of appealed prior-auth denials are overturned: the information existed and wasn’t used.

Avoided LUPAs · $14K / branch · plus auth write-offs (not yet measured)

Vendor must prove
  • Enforces all four ceilings as separate tests, not one
  • Puts payer rules in front of the clinician when the plan of care is created
  • Flags patients one visit short of the floor, but only for clinically indicated visits
  • Never adds a visit just to clear the threshold. 21% of claims just above it were non-compliant
The lock question

“Walk one patient through all four ceilings. What does the scheduler see when authorization and the LUPA floor conflict?”

Scored byLUPA rate & leakage · auth write-offs
IV
Waterfall

Delivery Throughput

Industry fill rates run 88–90%, so about one authorized visit in ten never gets staffed. This pays on the non-episodic book. Under episodic pay it protects coverage, not revenue.

Reduced premium / contract labor · $36K / branch

Vendor must prove
  • Fast backfill of cancellations and refusals
  • Paraprofessional pool planned apart from admissions capacity
  • Realistic travel claims: 5–15%. Divide a 20–40% claim by two or three
The lock question

“At your reference customer, what happened to visits per clinician per day compared with a branch that didn’t go live?”

Scored byMissed / rescheduled rate · premium labor %
V
Waterfall · conditional

Coordination Load

Three sources of load: the SOC and recert cycle, the per-discipline assignment burst, and chasing authorizations. The notification storm is a safety problem, not an annoyance.

Admin hours, only for work that should exist. Deleted work isn’t platform credit.

Vendor must prove
  • Filters notifications to state changes only
  • Sizes the load from real handle times in HCHB workflow records
  • Tells us what to delete, not just what to automate
  • Covers coordination time (gap CO-12)
The lock question

“Which of our workflows would your product remove rather than speed up?”

Scored byHand-off latency Not available todayTask census with handle time
VI
Waterfall

Workforce Stability

With 70% paid per visit, an unstable schedule means unstable pay. In a study of 3,716 nurses, full-time RNs with the most volatile schedules (75th percentile) were 16% more likely to quit.

Reduced turnover · $20K / branch · $1.6M network

Vendor must prove
  • Protects the clinician’s booked week, not just fills it
  • Measures volatility: variation in daily visit count over the trailing 28 days
  • Moves the ~30 minutes of unpaid evening confirmation calls into the system
The lock question

“How does your product make a clinician’s week less volatile, and how would we see that in 90 days of payroll?”

Posture DE-09

It assists where we said a human decides. Smart Scheduling overreaches on 16 variables. That’s how the Alabama pilot failed.

“Which decisions does your product make without a human?”

Evidence

Outcome metrics, not input metrics. “70% auto-scheduled” isn’t a result. No peer-reviewed study of any home health scheduling product exists.

“Show a controlled before-and-after, not one customer’s story.”

Integration

HCHB has no public API: integration runs on HL7v2 and CCD over SFTP. Its read/write FHIR marketplace opens in 2027.

“Who carries the integration cost, and what changes in 2027?”

Total cost

The license is about 10% of total cost. Internal labor is 42%. Implementation scales with branch count, not census.

“Price three years for 80 branches, trainer-days included.”

Data fidelity

Accurate constraints and visit durations matter more than the algorithm. Heavy scheduler overrides in the first 60 days sour adoption.

“What happens when schedulers override you 40% of the time?”

Where we stand, internally

Measure first. Decide the platform on evidence.

We’re funding a two-quarter phase to measure and configure. The platform decision comes at the gate. Vendors are being evaluated, not selected. Price HCHB Smart Scheduling as a costed comparator either way.

  1. Auth write-offs and LUPA leakage measured. If both come back small, the margin program stops.
  2. Configuration and standard work capture their share first. The platform only has to justify the rest.
  3. A baseline exists for Pillar I’s core metric.
  4. Override behaviour and data hygiene are shown to be manageable in one branch.
