# Initiative Build Process — Ten Steps

**How we thought about the Compassus Capacity & Scheduling initiative, built every document, and
arrived at a target state — written so a new initiative can be dropped in at Step 1 and built out.**

> **How to use this.** Each step has the same five parts:
>
> | Part | What it gives you |
> |---|---|
> | **The thinking** | How we reasoned about this step, and what we were trying to avoid |
> | **How we ran it** | The mechanics — who is in the room, what is on the table, how long |
> | **What it produced** | Every document that came out, and **exactly what went into each one** |
> | **What it hands forward** | The specific thing the next step cannot start without |
> | **▢ Run it on a new initiative** | Fill-in prompts. Copy the block, answer it, and you have run the step |
>
> The `▢` blocks are the reusable instrument. Everything above them is the worked example.

---

## The shape — this is not a straight line

Ten steps, but only Steps 1 and 10 are genuine endpoints. The middle is a **loop** with a
**parallel track** running beside it.

```
  STEP 1 ─ MEETINGS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
  the raw material — never stops, feeds every step below
      │
      ▼
  ┌──────────────────────── THE LOOP ─────────────────────────────┐
  │                                                                │
  │   STEP 2 ──▶ STEP 3 ──▶ STEP 4 ──▶ STEP 5 ──▶ STEP 6          │
  │   variable    current    bottlenecks  re-cut &    update       │
  │   list        state      & pain       prioritise  the flows    │
  │      ▲                                              │          │
  │      └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘          │
  │           each pass sharpens the one before it                 │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
      │                                     ║
      │                                     ║  STEP 7 — BUSINESS CASE
      │                                     ║  opens at the first loop pass,
      │                                     ║  then runs in parallel throughout
      ▼                                     ║
  STEP 8 ──▶ STEP 9 ──▶ STEP 10             ║
  what feeds   MVP spec   target            ║
  the work     + priority  state            ║
      ▲                                     ║
      └─────────────────────────────────────╝
        the case tells you what to build first
```

**Three things this diagram asserts, each learned the hard way:**

**1 · Steps 2–6 run more than once.** We ran the loop three full times. The variable list existed
*before* the current-state map, was corrected *by* the map, was re-cut after the bottleneck work,
and the flows were then redrawn against the re-cut list. A team that runs 2→3→4→5→6 once and moves
on ends up with a variable list describing a process nobody actually follows.

**2 · Step 7 does not wait.** The business case conversation opens the first time you can name a
*mechanism* — not after the design is done. Started late, it gets built backwards from a number
somebody wants to hear. Started early it does something more useful: **it tells you which variables
are worth building for**, which is exactly Step 9's input.

**3 · Step 8 is a dependency, not an appendix.** "What other platforms and processes feed this" looks
like an integration question. It is really the question of *whether the data you are designing
against exists at all* — and it re-enters the loop, because half of what you find is a new variable.

# STEP 1 — Meetings

### The thinking

Meetings are the raw material. Everything downstream is a rendering of something somebody said in a
room. So the design question is not *"should we meet"* — it is **what kind of meeting, in what
order, with whom, and what is on the table when it starts.**

We used four distinct meeting types and they are not interchangeable. Running the wrong type is the
most common way an initiative wastes its first month.

| Type | Purpose | What's on the table | Who must be there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** | Learn what happens | Nothing — a blank page and questions | The people whose hands are on the work |
| **SME download** | Get one expert's whole model | A recorder | One deep expert, uninterrupted |
| **Validation** | Correct a draft | **A draft, deliberately imperfect** | The system-of-record expert |
| **Decision** | Close open questions | The open-questions list | Someone who can actually decide |

**The rule that matters most: never bring a blank page to a validation meeting, and never bring a
draft to a discovery meeting.** People *generate* poorly and *correct* brilliantly. Bringing a draft
too early caps what you learn; bringing none too late wastes the expert.

### How we ran it

**Six sessions, in this order:**

1. **Full-day cross-functional discovery** (Jul) — scheduling operations, clinical staff,
   technology/data analysts. Open. Mapped referral intake through visit execution.
2. **Operator SME download** (Jul) — one field-clinical/operations leader, giving the whole mental
   model of what actually controls branch capacity.
3. **Role-perspective build** (Jul) — five roles worked through separately: Branch ED,
   DCS/Clinical Manager, Senior Scheduler, Field RN/SOC nurse, Workforce Strategist.
4. **On-site whiteboard validation** (13 Aug) — three seats, chosen deliberately:
   - the **author of the maps and variable list**, who owns every revision
   - the **system-of-record SME** — the person who knows what the software does when you click the
     thing. The highest-density source in the session
   - the **executive lead**, who owns vendor relationships and can decide in the room
5. **Asynchronous correction rounds** (17–18 Aug) — written, against specific sheets.
6. **Executive instrument review** (20 Aug) — one reviewer, on the vendor questionnaire, in writing.

**Three mechanics worth copying exactly:**

- **Keep a line-numbered verbatim transcript.** Every later claim cites a line number. Eight weeks
  on, "somebody said scheduling was the problem" is worthless; `[T:214]` is evidence.
- **Put a decision-maker in the room.** Ten decisions were registered on 13 August in one day. Without
  that seat they would have been ten email threads over a month.
- **Expect the correction to arrive after the meeting.** The single most important correction in the
  whole initiative came in *after* the on-site session, in writing, from the map's author:

  > Routine visits are **first plotted by the evaluating clinician at admission, not by the
  > scheduler.** In steady state there is **no scheduler workflow at all** unless a visit must be
  > reassigned.

  Everything drawn before that had the wrong actor holding the pen. Build a channel for the
  after-the-fact correction and treat it as proof the validation worked, not as a nuisance.

### What it produced

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Discovery session record** | The full-day session. Structured into: what schedulers actually do · the end-to-end workflow chain · capacity vs. scheduling · clinician dynamics · technology landscape · patient engagement · next steps · four named processes · a glossary |
| **Line-numbered transcript** | The raw whiteboard session, numbered so every `[T:###]` citation resolves |
| **Whiteboard session record** | Executive summary + decisions DE-01…DE-10 + named bottlenecks + adoption constraints + the post-session correction + deferrals |
| **Five role-perspective briefs** | One per role: what they optimise for, their tactics, where they conflict with the others |
| **Scheduler insight session agenda** | A question bank for the frontline session *(drafted; session not yet run)* |
| **Glossary** | SOC · ROC · DCS · TIC · POC · LUPA · buddy codes · points · Shift Finder · POSFC |

**The one output that changed everything else:** the discovery session's core finding.

> **The scheduling problem is not a scheduling problem.** Schedulers are administrators. Their only
> true scheduling decision is the start-of-care intake call. The real inefficiency is upstream —
> clinical documentation, DCS workflow, authorization holds, and above all capacity management.
> **Scheduling gets blamed because it is the last visible touchpoint.**

That finding is why Step 2's variable list has a *capacity* half at all.

### What it hands forward

A vocabulary, a cast of actors, a rough process spine, and a set of numbers people said out loud
(50–60 auth notifications/day · 7+ tasks per admission · 40–50 patients per RN+LPN pair · ~3,000
clinicians × ~30 min/day). **Every one of those numbers was carried forward labelled as an
estimate** — which is why none of them quietly became a business case line in Step 7.

# STEP 2 — Developing the variable list

### The thinking

**A variable is anything that changes the answer.** Not a feature, not a requirement, not a data
field — a *thing that varies and therefore constrains the decision.*

We built the variable list **before** the current-state map, deliberately, and that ordering is the
recommendation. The reason: a process map drawn first will only contain the variables the person
drawing it already thinks about. A variable list built first — from the meetings, from the
disciplines, from the constraints people complained about — forces the map to account for things
the mapper would have skipped.

**The framing question we used to generate rows:**

> *For one visit to happen, what must all be true at once?*

That question is what produced the insight that shaped the whole initiative: **a home visit is not
one constraint but a dozen that must all hold simultaneously** — discipline and licensure match
(SN, PT, OT, ST, MSW, HHA, none of which substitute for one another), authorization, LUPA
thresholds, drive time, caregiver availability, continuity, acuity. **And these constraints
conflict.** Continuity fights geography. Productivity fights acuity. And it is all dynamic.

### How we ran it

**Group into families first, then fill each family exhaustively.** We used four ID families, and the
IDs are the join key to every document produced afterward:

| Prefix | Family | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| `SH-` | **Shared spine** | What both capacity and scheduling depend on |
| `C-` | **Capacity** | How much work can this branch deliver? |
| `S-` | **Scheduling** | Which clinician, which day, which route? |
| `CO-` | **Coordination** | What makes the plan survive contact with the day? |

Within those, sub-categories: roster · availability · territory · reachability · visit weighting ·
targets & ceilings · committed load · open room · SOC capacity · specialty competency · ramp ·
per-diem buffer · on-call · demand inflow · plan-of-care orders · compliance windows ·
authorization · order & consent readiness · discipline & role match · specialty & acuity ·
clinician working pattern · clinician restrictions · patient availability · caregiver constraints ·
clinical timing · continuity · routing · week distribution · missed-visit handling · welcome call ·
availability capture · reminders · en-route · channel & preference · reschedule · coverage ·
urgent same-day · no-show · multi-discipline · care-team updates · coordination time load.

**The columns on each row** — this is the schema, and it is worth copying:

| Column | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| ID | Join key. **Never renumbered** |
| Variable name | Plain language, not system language |
| What it is | One sentence a branch director would recognise |
| Category / rollup | For the coverage view |
| **Constraint class** | Hard · Structural · Soft — decides whether it can be traded off |
| **MVP requirement** | Yes / Maybe / No |
| **Automation posture** | **Control · Assist · Read** — what we will permit a system to do |
| Current state | How it works today, or that it doesn't |
| **Notes / additional context** | Pain-point and road-bump commentary. *This is where the operational truth is* |

**The two columns people skip, and shouldn't:**

- **Automation posture, recorded per variable, in advance.** Decide what a system may decide
  *before* you meet a product that would like to decide it for you. In Step 7 this became the single
  most useful column we had: one candidate product scored highest overall **and overreached our
  stated posture on 16 variables.** Without the posture column that reads as a feature win. With it,
  it reads as a fit problem.
- **Notes.** Two free-text columns (`Notes`, `Additional Context`) carry the pain-point commentary.
  Every structured column tells you *what* the variable is; the notes tell you *why anyone cares*.

### What it produced

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Variable Inventory** — the spine | Meetings (Step 1) + the disciplines + the constraints people complained about + the "what must be true at once" generation question. **79 scored rows: 76 numbered + 3 unnumbered** |
| **The Concepts That Matter** | A 15-factor distillation of the inventory — the plain-terms version for people who will never open the full list |
| **Definitions & Concepts** | The controlled vocabulary underneath the inventory |
| **Big Picture** | Six plain-language groupings — Workforce · Capacity · Demand · Scheduling · Coordination · Results — with the primary variables under each. **The one-page explanation for someone who has five minutes** |
| **Variable backlog** | A running list of variables surfaced but **not yet numbered.** Append as new ones appear; work it down at each inventory update |

**Keep the backlog file.** New variables surface constantly — three gating-level ones (Insurance
Authorization, Add-On Orders, Clinician Safety) arrived during the 13 August session alone. Without
a backlog they either get lost or force a disruptive renumber.

### What it hands forward

The map in Step 3 now has a checklist to be drawn *against*, and the bottleneck work in Step 4 has
something to attach findings to. **Every later document — the flow sheets, the one-pager, the
vendor questionnaire's coverage grid, the business case workstreams — uses these IDs.** One
vocabulary, used everywhere, is worth more than three better ones used once each.

# STEP 3 — Mapping current state

### The thinking

The map's job is **not** to look good. It is to be *specific enough to be wrong in public* — so that
the person who knows better can point at a box and correct it.

Two decisions set the whole approach:

**Draw before you are ready.** A wrong map in the room beats a right map in a fortnight. Every sheet
we produced existed to be marked up.

**Current state only. No proposals, ever, on a current-state sheet.** Every footer says so. The
moment a current-state map carries an aspiration it stops being evidence and becomes an argument,
and people stop correcting it.

We also *rehearsed* before mapping the real thing. Four **illustrative scenario sheets** were built
first, from the variable list, with fictional patients and figures but real decision logic — one
clinician's day (five visits, 7.25 points against a ~7.0 ceiling), a 6:45am call-out recovery, a
branch leader's quarterly territory review. Their job was to make the variables *feel* like a real
day and to give the room something concrete to argue with. Registers do not convince anyone. A
worked day does.

### How we ran it

**Fix the design system before drawing anything.** Palette, type scale, block semantics, legend —
written down so nobody re-derives them. Ours became a reusable tool so the conventions survive
between sessions.

**Three conventions, and each one *is* a finding:**

| Convention | The rule | The finding it encodes |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour = actor** | A workflow item inside the EMR worked by a human carries the **human's** colour. The system colour appears only where the system acts by itself | When the legend was corrected, the sheets showed it plainly: **genuine automation in HCHB is approximately nil** |
| **Size = frequency** | Large block = happens every time · small block = conditional · pill = a watch condition, not a step | Weight on the page matches weight in the process |
| **Generated, never hand-drawn** | Sheets render from source scripts; the published PDF is never edited | Corrections arrive in bursts. A sheet you can't re-render in a minute goes stale |

**Split the process into flows rather than drawing one monster.** We drew one composite plus
separate flows, layerable:

| Sheet | What it carries |
|---|---|
| **Primary map** | The whole episode in four phases — the orientation sheet |
| **Detailed composite** | The original hand-built swimlane, redrawn with every correction |
| **SOC / ROC full flow** | The referral pass, then the per-discipline plan-of-care pattern |
| **Routine visits** | The clinician's own week, the day-before negotiation, the five dispositions |
| **Authorization** | Auth at its **two** interfaces: the gate at start of care, the ceiling inside the plan of care |
| **Recert & discharge** | End of episode, worked through staggered per-discipline discharges |
| **DCS / scheduler handoff** | The single most-cited breakdown, on one page |
| **Payer economics** *(added later, from Step 7)* | Why the same operational event costs differently in every payer class |

### What it produced

Beyond the sheets themselves:

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Process-facts file** | The distilled current-state truths every sheet draws from: admission · plan of care · auth's two interfaces · the clinician's own week · the five dispositions · missed-visit chain · capacity levers · recert & discharge · **and the binding drawing conventions.** Read before touching any sheet |
| **Redraw assessment** | The full working record, §1–26 — correction inventory, every conversational decision, the eight answers that closed the composite's open questions, and each sheet's build notes |
| **Flow-map tooling** | Palette, drawing primitives, the render/PDF build script, and a collision checklist — so the design system is never re-derived |

### What it hands forward

A picture specific enough that Step 4 can point at exactly where the process breaks — and a
correction history that proves the picture is trustworthy.

**The measure of done:** current state is finished when a session produces **no new corrections**.
Not when the drawing looks good. We counted 26 rounds.

# STEP 4 — Highlighting bottlenecks and significant pain points

### The thinking

This is the step where an initiative either earns its budget or becomes a wish list. Two separate
jobs happen here, and conflating them is the classic mistake:

- **A pain point** is what people complain about. It tells you where the energy is.
- **A bottleneck** is what constrains throughput. It tells you where the money is.

**They are frequently not the same thing** — and we found exactly that. The loudest complaint
(50–60 daily authorization notifications, mostly non-actionable) is real, exhausting, and **not the
binding constraint on growth.** The binding constraint is SOC-capable clinician availability, which
almost nobody complained about because it never presents as an event — it presents as a branch that
somehow never grows.

**The organising discipline of the whole step:** classify every constraint by **whether we can
change it, and who owns it.** Severity tells you what hurts. **Changeability tells you what to do.**

### How we ran it

**4a — The constraint register.** Every constraint on the work today, given an ID (`CN-01`…`CN-51`)
and classified:

| Class | Meaning | What you do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulatory** | A statutory or CoP requirement | Design around it. Non-negotiable |
| **Payer** | A contract or plan rule | Encode as *data*, never as code |
| **Vendor-configurable** | A setting that is off or wrong today | **Free win.** Separate, faster track |
| **Vendor product limit** | The incumbent system cannot do it | **This is the case for building** |
| **Our own policy** | A rule we wrote and can unwrite | **Free win.** No system needed |
| **Cultural** | How people work | Change management, not requirements |
| **Labour agreement** | Contractual with the workforce | A constraint on incentive design |

**The result was the single most valuable output of the entire initiative:**

> Of 51 constraints, **nine cannot be toggled** (`CN-22`…`CN-30`). **Those nine are the entire
> argument for building or buying something.** And four of the loudest pain points
> (`CN-18`, `CN-31`, `CN-32`, `CN-33`) turned out to be **our own policy** — they need no new system
> at all and were moved to a separate, faster track.

Several of the most painful things about the process were vendor design choices, not regulatory
requirements: routing every physician order to a clinical director for approval; hiding
pending-authorization visits from the clinician's calendar; blocking a nurse from handing a visit to
her own LPN; blocking an RN from seeing her supervisee's schedule. **Sorting the vendor's constraint
from the regulator's constraint is what tells you what you are actually buying.**

**4b — The bottleneck dossiers.** Twelve, ranked by leverage, each with the same seven fields:

`mechanism · evidence · downstream effects · what to measure · candidate remedies · open questions · rank`

The fixed schema is what makes them *comparable* rather than merely listed. The first four are the
ones worth solving first.

**4c — The connection points.** Where one function's decision constrains another's action — the
seams. Ten of them (`CP-1`…`CP-10`). **These are precisely where a manual system loses information,
and therefore where automation pays.** Three carry the initiative:

- **CP-3** — SOC capacity gates admission acceptance. **Capacity gates growth.** The highest-value
  connection point in the system.
- **CP-4** — a cancellation recovers capacity. Governs whether the branch wastes what it already has.
- **CP-5** — point totals feed caseload balance. Underlies both — **and points were undefined.**

### The two findings that reframed the work

**Some workflows should not be automated. They should not exist.** Said in the room:
*"That workflow shouldn't exist to begin with for the scheduler."* The per-discipline task
explosion — four disciplines producing four tasks, then four more at approval, eight clicks for a
decision already made — is not an automation opportunity. It is waste to delete. **Before
specifying automation for a task, ask whether the task is legitimate.**

**If you can't see it, you can't plan it.** Pending-authorization visits exist nowhere a clinician or
leader can see and count toward nothing. A visit lands on Thursday's calendar on Wednesday afternoon
because a scheduler was holding it in her head or on a sticky note. Compounding it, the system
generates a pending-auth workflow per patient per day — training schedulers to bulk-clear without
reading, **so the one that mattered gets cleared too.** That is a bottleneck, a requirement, a
metric and a test case in one paragraph. Push every finding until it reads like that.

### What it produced

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Constraint register** | The flow sheets + the meetings + the system-of-record SME. 51 constraints, each classified by changeability and owner |
| **Bottleneck dossiers** | The same sources, ranked by leverage. Twelve dossiers, seven fields each |
| **Connection-point register** | Derived from the capacity/scheduling split — the seams between the two functions |
| **The failure catalogue** | Every named failure, grouped by domain: capacity · scheduling · workforce · patient |

### What it hands forward

Step 5 now has a reason to re-cut the variable list — the bottlenecks say which variables actually
matter — and Step 7 has its mechanisms, because **every business case lever traces to a bottleneck
dossier.**

# STEP 5 — Modifying the variable list and prioritising

### The thinking

**This is the step that closes the loop back to Step 2, and it is the one most teams skip.**

The variable list from Step 2 was built from meetings. It is now three documents older: the map
corrected who does what, the bottleneck work said which variables carry weight, and the connection
points revealed variables that only exist *between* functions. The list must be re-cut against all
three — and then, critically, **scored**, because a list of 79 things is not a plan.

**Prioritisation has to be derived, not judged.** The temptation is to sit in a room and rank by
opinion. We scored instead, straight from the inventory columns, so the ranking is reproducible and
arguable at the *criterion* level rather than the conclusion level.

### How we ran it

**5a — Modify.** Three kinds of change:

| Change | Example from this initiative |
|---|---|
| **New variables** | Three gating-level ones arrived from one session alone: Insurance Authorization, Add-On Orders, Clinician Safety |
| **Corrected variables** | The routine-visit correction meant several scheduling variables changed owner from scheduler to clinician |
| **Renamed groupings** | The categories were renamed to match the target architecture agreed in the same session |

**Renaming is a real cost — pay it deliberately and document the lineage.** An earlier six-category
framing (Workforce · Capacity · Demand · Scheduling · Coordination · Results) preceded the current
one. We did not delete the earlier artifacts; we documented the relationship explicitly —
*"not a contradiction, a lineage"* — so nobody encountering an older sheet concludes the model
changed underneath them.

**5b — Score.** Three derived fields, computed from the inventory, not assigned by debate:

| Field | Rule | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Weight** | MVP required `Yes` = 3 · `Maybe` = 1 · `No` = 0 | Ordinary ranking |
| **Gating** | Constraint class is Hard **or** Structural **AND** MVP required = `Yes` | **A knockout.** A product that cannot do it is disqualified however well it scores elsewhere |
| **Conflict risk** | The vendor's built-in way of working could contradict how we operate | Predicts the adoption fight before you have it |

**The gating flag is worth more than any weighted average.** A weighted score ranks options. A
gating flag *eliminates* them — and in a vendor evaluation, elimination is the decision you actually
need to make.

**5c — Publish what you still don't know, in dependency order.** The output nobody expects and
everybody uses. Nine open questions, ordered so answering one unblocks the next:

> **Question 1 — the point system.** What a point represents; values by visit type and discipline;
> daily and weekly targets by clinician type; who sets them; how points relate to time; how travel
> is treated.
>
> **It is the shared currency of both capacity and scheduling. Referenced everywhere, defined
> nowhere.** Almost nothing downstream can be specified until it is pinned.

Then: capacity mechanics (SOC eligibility, the SOC↔routine exchange rate, caseload limits, planning
horizon, reserve policy) · scheduling mechanics (build sequence, last-minute-order decision tree,
fallback rules, urgency tiering) · constraints & rules · interfaces & data flow · economics.

**Ordering by dependency is the whole trick.** An unordered list of unknowns produces parallel
flailing. An ordered one tells you what to resource first.

# STEP 6 — Updating the flows as the conversation evolves

### The thinking

**This is a maintenance discipline, not an event** — and it is the step that decides whether the
whole body of work stays trustworthy or quietly rots.

The flows drawn in Step 3 are now wrong in specific ways. Step 4 found bottlenecks that belong on
them. Step 5 changed variable owners and names. New corrections keep arriving from people who saw
the sheet after the session. **Every one of those has to land on the sheet, or the sheet stops being
the shared reference and people go back to arguing from memory.**

The infrastructure decision that makes this survivable was taken back in Step 3: **generate, never
hand-draw.** A correction should cost a minute, not an afternoon. If updating the map is expensive,
it will not happen, and everything downstream drifts.

### How we ran it

**Twenty-six numbered correction rounds**, retained in full. A sample of what an actual round looks
like — these are not editorial tweaks, they are substantive reversals:

| Round | The correction | What it changed |
|---|---|---|
| Legend | Colour = actor; **the person beats the system** | Revealed that genuine automation in the EMR is ~nil |
| Actors | Routine visits are plotted by the **clinician**, not the scheduler | Reversed the owner on an entire flow |
| Dispositions | **Decline and reassign are different things** | Split one box into two |
| Timing | The five dispositions happen **the day before**, not at the visit | Moved a whole panel to a different phase |
| Ownership | Scheduler owns matching; **clinician owns confirmation** | Redrew the handoff |
| Auth | A new certification period is a **new authorization question** | Added a gate that did not exist on the sheet |
| Phasing | Phase 2 is plotted by frequency and assigned **in one pass** | Collapsed two steps into one |

**Three mechanics that made 26 rounds sustainable:**

1. **One file holds the distilled truth; the sheets are downstream of it.** A process-facts document
   carries every current-state fact and every drawing convention. **Read it before touching any
   sheet.** Corrections land there first, then propagate to the drawings.
2. **Keep the correction history, numbered.** It is your completeness metric *and* your audit trail.
   When someone asks "why does the sheet say that?", the answer is a round number and who said it.
3. **Switch off anything not yet stable.** The generators support printing variable IDs under each
   step. We turned that **off** until the IDs settled — printing unstable IDs on a sheet means
   somebody cites one that later changes. **Better blank than wrong.**

### The judgement call: when is it done?

> **Current state is finished when a session produces no new corrections.** Not when the drawing
> looks good, not when the deadline arrives, and not when you are tired of it.

Two failure modes to name, because both feel like progress:

- **Stopping early** — the map looks clean, so it ships. It is clean because it is vague.
- **Never stopping** — every session generates corrections because the scope keeps widening. Fix
  this by *bounding the flow*, not by refusing corrections. Record what you deliberately are not
  mapping.

We recorded the deferrals explicitly: no future-state map yet · no deep authorization-side mapping ·
no weekend or after-hours scheduling · no aide/MSW/ST scheduling paths · recert/ROC/new-order treated
as one trigger class, so per-trigger differences remain unrecorded. **A deferral you recorded is a
scope boundary. A deferral you didn't record is a surprise in month four.**

### What it produced

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Redraw assessment §1–26** | Every correction, every conversational decision, the eight answers that closed the composite's open questions, and each sheet's build notes |
| **Process-facts file** | The distilled current-state truths + the binding drawing conventions. The upstream source for every sheet |
| **Updated flow sheets** | All eight, regenerated |
| **The flow-map tooling** | Palette, drawing primitives, build script, collision checklist — captured so conventions survive between sessions and people |

### What it hands forward

A current-state picture nobody argues with — which is the only credible baseline for Step 10's
target state. **You cannot draw a target state against a current state people still dispute.**

# STEP 7 — Opening the business case, in parallel

> **This step does not sit between Steps 6 and 8. It starts during the first pass of the loop and
> runs alongside everything.** It is drawn as a parallel track in the diagram for that reason.

### The thinking

**Why parallel, and not at the end.** A business case written after the design is finished gets
built backwards from a number somebody wants to hear. A business case opened early does something
far more useful: **it tells you which variables are worth building for.** It is a prioritisation
instrument long before it is a funding instrument.

It also forces a question the design work will otherwise dodge: *if this works, what specifically
changes, in what unit, that somebody could count?* A bottleneck that cannot answer that is either
mis-stated or genuinely not worth solving.

**What the parallel track actually needs from the loop, and when:**

| From | It gives the case | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Step 1 meetings | The mechanisms and the raw quantities | Immediately |
| Step 4 bottlenecks | **Every lever traces to a dossier.** The dossier's "what to measure" is the case's unit | As dossiers complete |
| Step 5 scoring | Which levers are attached to gating variables | At each re-cut |
| Step 8 platforms | Whether the number can be *measured* today or must be built | Continuously |

### How we ran it

**7a — Learn how the money actually works, before sizing anything.** We built a payer and episode
economics reference plus a seven-part sourced research corpus. Five findings changed the *design*,
not just the case:

1. **Payment is per 30-day period** — not per certification period, not per visit. Two payment
   periods sit inside one 60-day certification period, each with its own case-mix group, payment
   amount and LUPA threshold. **A model that only knows the certification period cannot see the
   cliff it is walking toward.** That is a data-model requirement, discovered in an economics
   document.
2. **There is a ceiling as well as a floor.** Below the LUPA threshold the period pays per visit
   instead of in full. Above it, under episodic payment, every further visit is cost with no
   revenue. The target is *the clinically right number, above the floor and no higher than the
   period supports* — shown to the clinician, **never weighed against clinical need in an objective
   function.**
3. **The fastest win needs no new data.** The authorization team already writes payer rules into a
   coordination note at verification. Surfacing them at plan-of-care creation is a schema change and
   a surfacing point — not a data-gathering programme. And it is a patient-care win: abrupt
   discharges happen because nobody planned against the real visit budget.
4. **The book is majority non-episodic** — ~260M episodic against ~549M total. **Several levers
   invert by payer class.** Automating clinicians' unpaid evening confirmation calls creates
   sellable capacity only where clinicians are paid per visit; under episodic payment an extra visit
   earns nothing. Naming that distinction is what keeps the case credible.
5. **Policy is a clock you don't control.** A national moratorium on new Medicare home health
   enrollment meant growth could not be bought with new locations — **which converts this initiative
   from an efficiency programme into the growth strategy.** That is the strongest single framing
   available to a steering committee, and it came out of the research corpus.

**7b — Find the house format before drafting.** We did not invent a format. We found the one the
organisation already approves cases in — a worked internal example — and matched it exactly.

**Two panels.** *Left, the waterfall:* value drivers green, costs red, subtotals navy, read left to
right, subtotals breaking the run into named workstreams each with its own net. Costs drawn
explicitly and netted, never hidden — a zero-cost item is still drawn as a bar at zero. ROI stated
as a percentage against cost. *Right, the upside panel:* probabilistic or behaviour-dependent value,
each lever expressed as **a percentage of a named revenue base, with the base stated.** One lever is
allowed to be **"not yet valued"** with a named owner to fill it.

**The rule deciding which panel:** hard, countable, removable cost goes on the waterfall.
Probabilistic or behaviour-dependent value goes right.

Reading the approved example also handed us the *anchors* — the revenue bases and modelling
conventions finance had already blessed. And it revealed something worth a week of work: that case
already carried a placeholder naming *reduced LUPA risk and capacity for growth* as unvalued
productivity upside. **Our initiative's core ground was sitting in someone else's business case as
an unquantified footnote.**

**7c — Tier every case honestly.** No headline number without saying how much of it is real:

| Tier | Meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Committed** | A countable unit exists today; finance can book it | Waterfall, green |
| **Modelled** | Mechanism established, arithmetic sound, an input missing | Waterfall, green, **flagged** |
| **Directional** | Real and evidenced, size depends on unmeasured behaviour | Upside panel |
| **Not yet valued** | Named, credible, deliberately unpriced | Upside panel |
| **Risk avoided** | A loss that does not occur | **Narrative only. Never a bar** |

**7d — Write the register before the model.** Every financially connected case, direct or indirect,
each with five fields: **mechanism · evidence · sizing formula · tier · what must be supplied to
commit it.** Seven families: workforce & administrative cost (the hard core) · revenue capture &
leakage · utilisation & margin · growth & throughput (the largest upside) · quality-linked revenue ·
risk avoided · option value.

**Two entries worth studying as craft:**

*The largest hard lever* was published **with two honesty notes attached** — that not all of it is
attributable to the platform, because part of the reduction comes from workflow that arguably should
not exist at all; and that the release phases across the rollout, so year one carries a fraction.
Those two sentences are what make the headline believable.

*The LUPA lever* was published with an **absolute gate**: the recoverable share is *only* visits that
were clinically indicated and lost to an operational failure. Nothing in the case justifies adding a
visit to clear a threshold — and a federal audit found 21% of claims just above the threshold
non-compliant, with contractors targeting that cluster. **State the gate in the case itself, not in
a footnote.**

**7e — Write down the rules that remove money.**

- Where one lever sits *inside* another, **never add them.**
- Where two levers overlap on the same event, count it once.
- Levers that pay only on part of the book must not be applied to all of it.
- Growth revenue converts at *contribution* margin, not the blended rate, and must not be added to
  any lever already assuming the same freed capacity.

> A reviewer who finds a double-count you did not disclose discounts your whole case. A reviewer who
> finds the rule you wrote to prevent it trusts the rest of it.

**7f — Publish what you cannot yet know.** A table of inputs only finance and operations can supply,
**each row naming the case it converts from modelled to committed.** One was flagged as the
highest-value single ask — the **pay-model split across the estate** — because for a per-visit worker
cost is linear and optimisation cannot create margin, while for a salaried worker the marginal unit
is near-free. **The named pilot candidates were the per-visit sites: the best places for adoption
and the worst places for proving a margin case.** Naming that tension in advance is the difference
between a controlled trade-off and a failed pilot.

**7g — Say what the case must never claim.** Ours does, explicitly. This is what lets clinical
leadership support a financial document: no adding visits to clear a payment floor · margin never
enters a scheduling objective function or weighs against clinical need · fewer visits is not itself
the goal · **and no saving that depends on a specific manager working weekends** — if it is not
encoded as standard work, it is not a business case. We also killed one of our own levers on the
evidence, having found the effect was statistically insignificant.

**7h — Then build the model.** Last, not first. **One sheet of named, editable assumptions** feeding
both panels, so the case is argued at the assumption level rather than the conclusion level. The
"what must be supplied" table *is* the input block; everything else is a formula.

> A sponsor who disagrees with the headline and can't see why, disagrees with **you**. A sponsor who
> can change one assumption and watch the number move is **negotiating assumptions** — which is the
> conversation you want.

|---|---|
| **Payer & episode economics reference** | How payment works, the payer classes, the floors and ceilings, the authorization object |
| **Research corpus** (7 parts) | Rate mechanics · managed care · commercial & Medicaid · utilisation & margin · cost & labour · authorization operations · value-based & policy. Every figure sourced and dated |

# STEP 8 — Defining the platforms and processes that feed the initiative

### The thinking

This looks like an IT integration question. **It is actually the question of whether the data you
have been designing against exists at all.**

Three distinct questions live here, and they need separate instruments:

| Question | Instrument |
|---|---|
| *What systems and processes touch this work?* | The landscape map |
| *For each data element we need — where does it actually live, who can pull it, how fresh is it?* | The source war-list |
| *What is our design assuming exists that in fact does not?* | The coverage / gap scan |

**The third one is the dangerous one**, because a design silently assumes availability. Our
capacity model treated a referral as ready-to-assign. The discovery's central finding was that a
visit is typically stuck upstream — in clinical review, pending authorization, a plan-of-care lock,
or a documentation hold — **and none of those states existed anywhere in the data model.** A capacity
tool that cannot see that gauntlet **shows green capacity while admissions stall upstream** — which
is precisely the blame-lands-on-scheduling trap the whole initiative exists to fix.

### How we ran it

**8a — Map the landscape, with the honest status of each.** Not a logo diagram — what each system
does, and *whether it is real-time*:

| System | Role | The status that matters |
|---|---|---|
| **HCHB** | Core clinical & workflow system of record | **Not real-time. Requires manual sync** |
| **Commure** | Intake / referral management | New. Does not yet push real-time status to schedulers |
| **NestMed** | Clinical documentation | Real-time — a significant improvement on point-of-care devices |
| **Pulse** | Utilisation review | Used by clinical review for frequency management |
| **Workday** | HR / PTO | **Integration to HCHB exists and is switched OFF.** PTO is hand-keyed |
| **External coding vendor** | ICD-10 coding | Discrepancies create plan-of-care holds that block scheduling |
| **Circadia** | AI welcome calls | In evaluation, some locations |

**The most valuable column is the status column.** "Integration exists and is off" is a free win
sitting in plain sight — turning it on removes manual entry and prevents a whole class of capacity
failure (five of seven nurses approved off the same day). That went straight onto the business case
as a committed, near-zero-cost lever.

**8b — Build the source war-list.** Every data element the tool needs, as **a row to be sourced.**
The columns are the point:

| Column | Who fills it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Data element | pre-filled | What we need |
| **Candidate source** | pre-filled | **A hypothesis to confirm or replace — not a finding** |
| Confirmed source system | the team | Where it really lives |
| **Report / table / screen** | the team | **The exact artifact.** "It's in HCHB" is not an answer |
| **Owner** | the team | The person who can actually pull it |
| Refresh cadence | the team | How fresh it must be |
| **Exists today?** | the team | **Y / Partial / N** |

> **Everything that comes back N or Partial is the build backlog.** That is the entire output of the
> instrument.

**Priority key:** `P0` = a policy decision that gates everything · `P1` = core feed for v1 ·
`P2` = needed for full function · `P3` = reference layer · `—` = derived, no source needed.

**The P0 rows are not data problems. They are policy decisions wearing data clothing.** Ours were:
the point-earning rules (visit type × discipline × weight), the expected-points derivation by FTE and
discipline, and the productivity thresholds. **Nothing the tool displays is trustworthy until those
are agreed** — and no amount of integration work substitutes for someone deciding them.

Note also the *derived* section: fields the tool computes rather than sources. Listing them stops the
team hunting for a source that does not and should not exist.

**8c — Run the coverage / gap scan.** Diff what the design assumes against what the discovery
established. Structure the diff around the actual value chain, and mark where the white space sits:

```
DEMAND ──▶ [READINESS gauntlet] ──▶ SUPPLY ──▶ [CONSTRAINTS] ──▶ DECISION ──▶ FEEDBACK
referrals   review / auth / POC     capacity   geo · acuity ·   accept /     economics ·
+ forecast  / documentation / TIC   measure    licensure        assign       quality · retention
   ▲ GAP          ▲ GAP                ✓ strong    ~ partial        ✓ ok        ▲ GAP
```

**The finding: we were strong in the middle and blind at both ends.** Strong at supply measurement
and matching. Blind on the *readiness gauntlet upstream* and blind on the *economic, quality and
retention feedback* that tells you whether a capacity decision was actually any good.

Three structural gaps came out of it, and each is a change of answer rather than a nice-to-have:

- **The readiness gauntlet** — review status, authorization status, plan-of-care lock, documentation
  holds, and the referral→first-visit clock. Attach as a *readiness state* on the referral.
- **The economic layer** — the model spoke in points and visits, never dollars. Missing: threshold
  risk, margin per period, and premium-labour spend as the cost of covering a gap. **Every executive
  capacity decision is ultimately economic.** *(This gap is exactly what Step 7 was filling in
  parallel — the two tracks met here.)*
- **Quality and compliance guardrails** — value-based measures, assessment timeliness, and the
  compliance windows, which are **hard scheduling constraints, not soft goals.**

Plus a plain correctness bug the scan caught: the matcher ranked per-diem clinicians by capacity and
proximity **but did not enforce their stated restrictions** ("No SOC", "No wound care", "No
high-acuity") — so it could mis-route. **Coverage scans find bugs, not just gaps.**

### What it produced

| Document | What went into it |
|---|---|
| **Technology landscape** | The discovery session — each system, its role, and its real-time status |
| **Data index** | The full data dictionary, domains A–J — field-level planning |
| **Source war-list** (`.md` + `.csv`) | Every element as a sourceable row. **The CSV is the fill-in instrument; the team edits it in a working session** |
| **Mockup / as-built data spec** | What the built tool's data model actually is, versus what was specified |
| **Ecosystem coverage scan** | The data index diffed against the discovery. Tiered: structural gaps that change the answer, versus extensions |

### What it hands forward

Step 9 now knows **what is buildable in v1 versus what needs a data programme first** — which is the
single biggest determinant of MVP scope. And Step 7 gets told which of its numbers can be *measured*
today, which converts modelled cases to committed ones.

# STEP 9 — Developing the MVP spec sheet with priorities

> **Status: written 22 Aug 2026** — [`mvp-spec-sheet.md`](../agents/compassus-capacity-pm/artifacts/mvp-spec-sheet.md).
> 19 of 79 variables in scope. The derivation below is what produced it.

### The thinking

**The MVP spec is not a new document — it is a projection of the variable list through three
filters.** If Steps 2, 5, 7 and 8 were done properly, writing it is mostly mechanical. If it feels
like it requires fresh invention, one of those steps is incomplete and that is where to go back to.

The three filters, applied in this order:

```
   ALL VARIABLES  (79)
        │
        ├─ filter 1 ─▶  MVP-REQUIRED?        from Step 5 scoring
        │               yes(3) / maybe(1) / no(0)
        │
        ├─ filter 2 ─▶  SOURCEABLE IN V1?    from Step 8 war-list
        │               exists today Y / Partial / N
        │
        └─ filter 3 ─▶  WORTH IT?            from Step 7 register
                        does a lever depend on this variable?
                                │
                                ▼
                        THE MVP SPEC SHEET
```

**And one hard constraint sitting over all three, decided in Step 5's session:**

> **Phase 1 is visualisation only — no automation in the first release.**

That single decision does most of the scoping work. It is also the direct answer to the previous
failed attempt: an optimiser built on an unmeasured foundation is what failed before. **The first job
of the product is not to optimise anything. It is to make capacity measurable and observable.**

**The framing that makes an MVP defensible:** state what you are deliberately *not* solving, in the
spec, in plain terms. Ours is *"the MVP does not build the schedule."* It gives schedulers and
leaders visibility they have never had. **The value is not the schedule — it is the measurement.**

### The inputs, already assembled

| Input | From | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Scored variable inventory | Step 5 | The MVP weight and the **gating** flags |
| Gating variables | Step 5 | Non-negotiable. Anything failing these is out of scope for v1, or the vendor is out |
| Source war-list | Step 8 | Which variables can actually be fed on day one |
| P0 policy decisions | Step 8 | **What must be decided before v1 means anything** |
| Business case register | Step 7 | Which variables carry a lever, and how big |
| MVP capture assumption | Step 7 | The share of full-product value the MVP is modelled to realise |
| Decisions register | Step 5 session | The scope boundaries already agreed |
| Coverage grid categories | market step | The eleven areas the spec should roll up to |

### How to run it

1. **Sort the inventory by weight, then flag every gating variable.** That is the candidate list, and
   it is derived, not debated.
2. **Cross it against the war-list.** A high-weight variable with no source is not v1 — it is a data
   programme with a date. Say so explicitly rather than quietly dropping it.
3. **Cross it against the business case.** A variable no lever depends on is a candidate for v2,
   however satisfying it would be to build.
4. **Apply the phase-1 posture.** Under visualisation-only, anything whose posture is *control* moves
   out of v1 by definition. This is a fast, large cut.
5. **Write the "not in v1" list as a first-class section**, with the reason and the trigger that
   would bring each item in.
6. **State the MVP's job in one sentence** that does not contain the word "optimise."
7. **Attach the P0 decisions as prerequisites**, with owners and dates, at the top of the spec.

### What it produced, and what the derivation found

Four filters over 79 scored rows left **19 in scope** — the Shared and Capacity layers only, MVP
required, sourceable, with a phase-1 posture override. **11 of the 19 are gating.**

**Two findings the arithmetic produced that judgment would have missed:**

**1 · The posture override is the whole discipline of the release.** Fifteen of the nineteen
in-scope variables are scored `Control` — the system *could* decide and act. **In phase 1 none of
them do; all nineteen ship at `Read`.** The scored posture is the *target-state ceiling*, not a v1
commitment. A team reading the inventory as a v1 automation spec has misread it — and that misread
is precisely what failed last time.

**2 · The MVP has a single point of failure, and it is a decision, not a build.** The four derived
variables that *are* the MVP's output — committed load, remaining capacity by day, week open
capacity, and assessing capacity by discipline — are all computed from two variables that are
**undecided policy**: the point values per visit type, and the productivity target and ceiling.

```
   SH-07 point values ──┐
                        ├──▶ C-05 ──┬──▶ C-06  remaining capacity by day
   SH-08 target/ceiling ┘           ├──▶ C-07  week open capacity
                                    └──▶ C-08  assessing capacity  ★ the growth number
```

> **Until those two are decided, the MVP has nothing to display.** This is the same conclusion as
> Step 5's open question #1, reached independently from the data model — which is a good sign the
> chain held. It has had no named owner since 17 August, and it is the largest risk to the release.

**This is why Step 9 is a projection and not an invention.** The scope, the knockouts, the critical
path and the honest statement of which business case levers v1 can and cannot move all fell out of
columns that already existed. **If writing the MVP spec requires fresh invention, one of Steps 2, 5,
7 or 8 is incomplete — go back there.**

# STEP 10 — Mapping the future / target state

> **Status: drawn 22 Aug 2026** — `Flow-Target-State.pdf`. Deferred through 26 correction rounds
> until current state stopped moving, which was the precondition.

### The thinking

**Why this is last, and why deferring it was right.** A target state drawn before the current state
stops moving encodes *your assumptions* instead of *the organisation's reality*. We deferred it
explicitly and recorded the deferral. The current-state work then produced 26 rounds of corrections —
every one of which would have been baked into a premature target state as a false premise.

**The target state is constrained, not free.** It has to be drawn against four things already
decided, and the drawing's real job is to make those constraints *visible as design*:

| Constraint | Source | What it forces |
|---|---|---|
| The **regulatory** constraints | Step 4 register | Design around. Non-negotiable, and they stay in the target state |
| The **immovable product limits** | Step 4 register | These are the reason the target state differs from today at all |
| The **automation posture** per variable | Step 2 / 5 | Decides, per box, whether the system drives, assists or only reads |
| The **module architecture** | Step 5 session | The shape the target state has to fit |

**The design principle that came out of the failure analysis**, and that the target state must
embody at every box:

> **The tool recommends; the human accepts.** People supply their own availability and preferences.
> The system does not drop work onto a calendar unilaterally.

This is not a nicety. The previous attempt failed because leadership permitted rejection of machine
assignments, and the smart logic was effectively pulled back out. **Design for the tenured
practitioner who does not want this. If the target state does not survive that person, it does not
survive.**

### The inputs, already assembled

| Input | From | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Current-state sheets | Steps 3 & 6 | The baseline. Draw the target in the **same visual language** so they can be laid side by side |
| Process-facts file | Step 6 | What must remain true |
| Constraint register | Step 4 | What must be designed *around* versus what may be designed *away* |
| Connection points | Step 4 | The seams the target state has to close |
| Module architecture | Step 5 | The frame |
| Automation posture | Step 2 / 5 | Per-box authority |
| MVP spec | Step 9 | Which parts of the target state are phase 1 versus later |
| "Workflows that should not exist" | Step 4 | **Boxes to delete, not redesign** |

### How to run it

1. **Draw it in the same visual language as current state** — same palette, same size semantics, same
   actor colours. The comparison is the point; a different style destroys it.
2. **Mark every box with its phase.** The target state is not one state — it is at least MVP and
   post-MVP, and probably three horizons.
3. **Delete before you redesign.** Start with the workflows Step 4 identified as illegitimate. A box
   removed is worth more than a box automated.
4. **Show the posture on the sheet.** Where the system controls, where it assists, where it only reads.
   That is the single most contested thing in the whole design; put it in the drawing.
5. **Close the connection points explicitly.** Each `CP-` from Step 4 should be visibly resolved, or
   visibly still open, in the target state.
6. **Keep the regulatory constraints visible.** A target state that has quietly dropped a compliance
   window is not a target state, it is a liability.
7. **Validate it the same way current state was validated** — bring the draft to the people who do
   the work and count the correction rounds. **Target states get corrected too.**

### What it produced, and the two choices that mattered

**Two markers per box, not one.** Every block carries `RELEASE · POSTURE` — `MVP · READ`,
`V2 · ASSIST`, `V3 · CONTROL`. One marker would not have done: release answers *when*, posture
answers *how far the system may go*, and they move independently. Posture is the contested question
in this initiative, so it belongs in the drawing rather than in a document nobody opens next to the
wall sheet. The key panel carries the line the sheet exists to make unmissable:

> **15 of the 19 MVP variables are *scored* control. In phase 1 every one ships at `READ` — the
> score is a ceiling, not a commitment.**

**Six boxes deliberately not drawn.** The rule about never filling a gap with a plausible box binds
harder on a target state than on a current state, **because there is no operational reality to
correct it against.** Rather than guess, the sheet carries an `OPEN · NOT DRAWN` panel listing what
the operator still has to close — where the tool sits relative to the intake platform, whether the
capacity read is a gate or advice, who owns the number day to day, and three more. Two of the six
were already on record as executive decisions carried elsewhere; three surfaced while drawing,
because each was a place the sheet could have been drawn two defensible ways.

**And the honest caveat, recorded on the sheet's own record:** current state earned its authority
through 26 correction rounds. **The target state has had none.** It must be walked with the operator
and the system-of-record SME the same way before it is treated as settled.

## The ten things that made this work

1. **Never bring a blank page to a validation meeting, or a draft to a discovery meeting.** People
   generate poorly and correct brilliantly.
2. **Build the variable list before the map.** A map drawn first only contains what the mapper
   already thinks about.
3. **Record the automation posture per variable, before meeting any vendor.** It is what turns a
   high score into a visible fit problem.
4. **Classify constraints by changeability, not severity.** Severity says what hurts; changeability
   says what to do — and separates the free wins from the build case.
5. **The loudest pain point is usually not the binding constraint.** Find both. They fund different
   things.
6. **Some workflows should be deleted, not automated.** Ask whether the task is legitimate before
   specifying automation for it.
7. **Run the loop more than once.** Three passes. The variable list, the map and the bottlenecks each
   correct the other two.
8. **Open the business case early and run it in parallel.** Late, it is built backwards from a wanted
   number. Early, it tells you what to build.
9. **Publish what you cannot yet know** — open questions in dependency order, missing inputs naming
   the case each unlocks, estimates labelled as estimates. Credibility comes from what you decline
   to assert.
10. **Current state is done when a session produces no corrections.** Not when it looks good. Count
    the rounds.
